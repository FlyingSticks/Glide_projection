Klein’s Erlangen program · the plane

Euclidean ⊂ Metric ⊂ Affine ⊂ Perspective

Four geometries, one nested family of transformation groups. Each inner group is a subgroup of the next; the further out you go, the fewer things stay fixed.

What each level fixes

Euclidean Rx + b,  RᵀR = I 3 absolute length and area — the scale is nailed down; figures are congruent, not merely similar

Each row also keeps everything above it. Going the other way — outward — those same items are exactly what breaks.

One idea underneath

From the outside in, every level is the projective plane plus a distinguished figure held fixed:

Fix a line — the line at infinity ℓ∞ — and you get affine geometry. “Parallel” now means “meeting on ℓ∞,” and the cross-ratio of three finite points with the point at infinity collapses to their simple ratio.

Fix two conjugate points on it — the circular points I, J = (1 : ±i : 0) — and you get metric geometry. Angle becomes a cross-ratio taken against the isotropic lines to I and J (Laguerre), and a circle is simply a conic through I and J.

Fix the scale and you get Euclidean geometry.

So the whole tower is one geometry with progressively more structure pinned down — and the cross-ratio, invariant of the outermost group, is what every inner ratio and angle is ultimately built from.

† On “metric.” Here it denotes the similarity level — angles and length ratios recoverable, absolute scale free — following the stratification common in computer vision (Hartley & Zisserman). Some classical texts reserve “metric geometry” for the full Euclidean metric; in that reading the middle two levels merge into three.

‡ Why Euler, not Venn. A strict inclusion chain E ⊂ Sim ⊂ Aff ⊂ Proj draws as four nested curves — an Euler diagram. A Venn diagram would insist on all sixteen regions, fifteen of them empty.

§ Dimension. Shown for the plane; the same tower holds in ℝⁿ, with the counts 3·4·6·8 becoming isometry n(n+1)/2, +1 for similarity, affine n(n+1), and projective (n+1)²−1.
