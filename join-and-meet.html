Join & Meet

A join is the line through two points — the mark the straightedge actually makes. A meet is the point where two lines cross — not drawn, found. Between them they are the entire repertoire of the tool, and each is the other read backwards.

The two moves

A join takes two points and returns the line through them, written A ∨ B. This is the straightedge's only mark-making act: lay the edge against two marks, draw. A meet takes two lines and returns the point they share, written a ∧ b. Nothing is drawn for a meet — the crossing is already there once both lines are; you only notice it.

So the two run in opposite directions: join manufactures a line out of points, meet extracts a point out of lines. Alternate them — join to get new lines, meet to get new points, repeat — and you have every straightedge construction there is, the complete quadrangle and its equal-spacing ladder included.

Why they are dual, and not merely opposite

Two operations are opposite if one undoes the other. These do not undo each other; they are dual, which is stronger and stranger. Take any true statement of incidence, swap every "point" for "line" and every "join" for "meet," and what results is another true statement — a different theorem, proved for free. The dictionary above is that swap in miniature, and each row is its own mirror: reading it right-to-left gives the same dictionary back.

Duality is why projective results arrive in matched pairs: Desargues' theorem converts into its own converse, Pappus into a partner, the complete quadrangle into the complete quadrilateral. Two vocabularies describe one geometry, and neither has a claim to being the original — which is the same indifference the pairing of vectors and covectors runs on, appearing here one level down, at the level of the tool.

What duality costs, and who pays

The symmetry is exact only in the projective plane. On an ordinary drafting sheet the two rows disagree: two points always join, but two parallel lines never meet, so "two lines determine a point" fails and the mirror cracks. Completing the plane with a line at infinity repairs it — parallels meet at their shared point at infinity, every pair of lines has a meet, and duality holds without exception. Panel II is where you can watch the repair being needed: swing one line toward parallel and the meet runs off the page rather than disappearing.

Which reverses the usual telling. The point at infinity is not an exotic addition to geometry; it is the price of keeping join and meet symmetrical, and it was owed the moment the straightedge had two moves instead of one.

Join makes a line from two points; meet makes a point from two lines. Neither is the primitive. The straightedge has been performing a duality every time it touches the page.
