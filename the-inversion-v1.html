The inversion — one congruence, two roles

Are parallel rays capturing a converging shape, or are converging rays capturing a rectilinear shape? Both — and the choice is the organizing move of the whole family. A projection is a line congruence given a role: used as edges under parallel light it is the glide (reading 1, right — convergent shape, rectilinear projection); used as rays over a rectilinear box it is the cross-slit (reading 2, left — rectilinear shape, convergent projection). The drawn armature is the shadow of the congruence, shared by both readings; the graduations differ. Drag the marker in the square to choose the congruence — at the corners the two readings fuse; off them, one armature splits into the dual pair.

Read the middle column twice. The drawn lines — the shadow armature — agree wherever the congruence degenerates: the whole diagonal (pencils), the r = 0 edges (a slit at infinity), and the corners. The full maps — lines and graduation and fibers together — agree only at the corners: perspective, orthographic, the two pushbrooms. Between those two levels lives the whole drama of the family: one congruence, worn two ways, drawing the same lines and measuring them differently. That is the inversion, and every sheet on this board is a chapter of it.
