One congruence, two readings — rays versus edges

Imagine constructing a perspective. You fix a station point — the eye — and a picture plane standing between the eye and the scene. From each point of the scene a single straight ray runs to the eye, and wherever that ray pierces the picture plane is where the point is drawn. Every ray passes through the one station point, so the whole image is a pencil of lines gathered at the eye — and that shared point is what makes distant things small and sends parallel lines to a common vanishing point. Now keep the picture plane and put the station point away. What remains is a two-parameter family of sightlines — a congruence — and it can be read two ways. Read it as rays and you get a camera: the two-slit, every sightline threading a pair of axes at different depths. Read it as edges and you get a drawing: each line of the picture carries a graduation, and depth is a mark sliding along it. Same lines, two readings. They agree exactly on a seam — and the seam turns out to be the entire classical inventory: orthographic, pushbroom, pinhole, the affine and projective rungs of Klein's ladder. Step off the seam and the readings split. You are then holding a projection that draws in straight, graduated edges but is not a camera and does not live on the ladder. That object is the subject of this board. Nothing here is asserted on trust: every page re-runs its own machine checks as you drag.

THE BOARD'S GLIDE — equal stations, and the diagonals bend  ·  the same shape, the same marks, two instruments  ·  tap to open the plate
THE CLAIM. Projections come in three grades. Real ones a camera can perform — perspective, the cross-slit — every meeting set finite. Ideal ones are exact limits of cameras — orthographic, the pushbroom. And constructed ones exist only on the drafting board: their rays bend, no camera performs them and none is the limit of cameras — yet they are fully lawful, and they do things no camera can. This site is the case for taking the third grade seriously. Every geometric statement on it links to a machine-checked proof.
