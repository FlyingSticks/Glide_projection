The other way to pull a station point apart. Sheet 37 split it into two slits at two depths on the same axis of sight — a cross-slit. This sheet splits it into two perpendicular armature axes: a vanishing axis that meters depth and a directing axis that assigns direction (Glide Drawings, pp. 88–90). One control: at k = 0 the map is identically the pinhole at Zp; as k rises, each vertical plane of orthogonals keeps its own vanishing point sliding out along the horizon, while each horizontal plane converges toward a directing point beyond the plane of infinity — intercepts coupled by 1/k + 1/c = 1, so the image of infinity opens from a point into a line.

Read the trade against the cross-slit. Both spend perspective's depth fidelity to buy breadth — but differently. The cross-slit halves the splay and pays with bowed depth lines and a drifting gauge; the glide takes the splay all the way to 0% while its depth lines stay dead straight and its gauge square images square at every k — no anamorphic correction. The price is honest too: at k = 1 width stops encoding depth altogether (only height recedes), and the far wall never pinches — try the deep corridor, which turns into a parallel slot and clearly belongs to perspective. The house plan bird's-eye and the site relief are the glide's home ground — plans stay plans. The ◫ ghost button lets the two sheets talk: it draws Sheet 37's cross-slit — the same station point pulled apart the other way, D = 100·k — in blue over the same scene, so one control drives both splittings at once and the two generalizations diverge from a common origin before your eyes.

The morph is exact: u = X·(k·p(Zg) + (1−k)·p(Z)), v = Y·p(Z) with p(Z) = −Zp/(Z−Zp) — width anchored true at the reference plane Zg, exactly the book's staging rectangle laid over the plan. At k = 0 it is the pinhole at Zp to machine precision; at k = 1 the width is parallel. Orthogonals draw straight to their vanishing point at k·x̂ on the horizon and continue to a directing point at c·ŷ beyond, 1/k + 1/c = 1. At k = 0 the blue ghost, the glide, and the pinhole are one and the same map. …
