Three Tools, Three Strata

A ruler is a straightedge with evenly-spaced markings brought in from the outside. That sentence is the entire stratification of geometry, stated as shop practice — and "brought in from the outside" is a theorem, not a manufacturing detail.

The decomposition

Ruler = straightedge + external markings is exactly incidence + affine datum. The straightedge draws joins and intersections and can never do more: Panel I shows why. Every candidate line through P meets ℓ somewhere, and rotating the candidate only chases the meeting point along; nothing constructible distinguishes the one line that misses. That the graduation must arrive from elsewhere is Playfair's independence — the parallel axiom cannot be derived from incidence — worn as shop practice. The drafting teacher's phrase and the logician's theorem are one statement.

What the markings are

Once imported, look at them: evenly spaced, endless in principle, numbers printed ascending. The graduation is a covector, restricted to the tool's edge — gap, evenness, marked ascent, all three, etched in aluminum. A ruler is a straightedge carrying one covector along itself, and using it is the standing vocabulary verbatim: laying the ruler against a line is parking the instrument, reading off a length is the pairing, and the etched unit interval is the gap, physically present.

The economics of the import

The outside purchase happens once. Panel II stamps a single gap — levels 0 and 1, one parallelogram's worth of data, the same gift as a marked midpoint in another currency — and from there the straightedge propagates it without limit: every further level, every subdivision, every refinement, the whole endless ruling of the plane. No second ruler is ever needed, which is why a drafting board carries one scale and many straightedges rather than the reverse. Slide the shear and watch the readouts hold: everything the graduation grants is stated in counts, and counts are shear-proof.

What even the ruler cannot do

Laid along one line and then along an oblique one, the claim that its marks "mean the same" in both positions is a further import — the compass's cargo, the circle. Panel III poses the question the affine page cannot answer, lets the compass answer it, and then shears the page: the circle goes elliptical and the cross-direction verdict leaves the page, while both rulings keep their internal evenness and every count survives. Three tools, three strata — straightedge, graduation, compass; projective, affine, metric. The covector project runs entirely on the first two. The third is the one it has been showing you how to decline.

One purchase, then incidence does the rest — and the shear is the assay that shows which claims were bought where.
