The polarity primer — point to line, out and back, one dimension down

One symmetric matrix, seen twice. Its zero set is the conic (here a circle of radius R); its action sends the draggable point P to the gold polar line C·P — the grade-reversing move: a grade-0 thing in, a grade-1 thing out. Drag P and watch: outside the circle, the polar is the chord joining the two tangency points of the tangents from P; on the circle, it is the tangent there; drifting toward the center, it recedes to infinity. The polar always stands perpendicular to OP through the reciprocal point at distance R²/|OP| — reciprocal distances, the 1/r signature. The secant shows the deeper fact: it cuts the conic at X₁, X₂, and the fourth harmonic point H of P — cross-ratio −1, the complete-quadrilateral machinery — lands on the polar, on every secant, always. Toggle Q for reciprocity: put Q on the polar of P and P is instantly on the polar of Q. Out and back returns home: that involutivity is exactly what forces the matrix symmetric, and is what earns the name polarity.

This is the whole of "every polarity is the polar map of a quadric," one dimension down where it fits on a sheet. The symmetric matrix C = diag(1, 1, −R²) does both jobs: the points with p·Cp = 0 are the circle, and the map p ↦ Cp is the polar. Points on the circle are exactly the self-conjugate ones — lying on their own polar, which is then the tangent. The harmonic point H on the ray OP is the classical inverse point in the circle — no relation to the board's "the inversion," which names the two-readings map; the collision of names is centuries old and worth flagging once. One dimension up, this same picture is stage C of the pairing plate: the quadric becomes the ruled surface through both slits, the polar of an axis point becomes the gold stack plane, and the reciprocal-distance law becomes the midplane involution g(ζ) with the slits as its fixed points.
