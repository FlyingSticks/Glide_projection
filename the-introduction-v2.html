The inversion — an introduction

What this system is, what it is built on, where its edges are, and what remains open. Written to be accurate about its own reach rather than to oversell it.

A projection is a line congruence given a role. The catalog of congruences is small — the parallel bundle, the pencil through a point, and the two-axis family. The two roles are rays (the family that flattens) and edges (the family that shapes the solid). The organizing move is the inversion: swap the role on one and the same congruence. Read as edges under parallel light, it is the glide — a convergent shape under rectilinear projection. Read as rays over a rectilinear box, it is the cross-slit — a rectilinear shape under convergent projection. One congruence, one drawn armature, two graduations.

What the system is

Orthographic, affine, perspective, pushbroom, cross-slit and glide are usually taught as six separate constructions. This board treats them as one structure seen from different sides. The single principle is the inversion above; from it the six fall out as either fixed points of the role-swap (where both readings agree as maps) or dual pairs (where one drawn armature carries two different projections). The drawing you make — the armature of picture points, vanishing points and directing points — is the orthographic shadow of the congruence, and it is shared by both readings whichever way you read it.

The four things it was built to be

The project set out to bring the six projections under one idea that (1) makes the geometry and mathematics work together, (2) can be modelled on computer, (3) is internally consistent, and (4) is explainable on its own terms while built on outside concepts. On all four it holds. The inversion is a single principle from which the family follows. It is modelled — every operator in it is running code, and the viewer ingests real geometry. It is internally consistent in the strong sense that the consistency is checked, not merely asserted. And it is explainable from its own axioms while resting on genuinely external foundations, named below.

What is verified, and what is not

A firm line runs through this work, and it is worth stating plainly. The geometry is machine-solid: the channel law, the two-plane interpolation, the factorization glide = Ortho ∘ Relief, the cross-slit camera on the same congruence, the straightness swap, the fixed-point classification of the corners, and the two nested agreement loci. None of these is in doubt at the precision the checks reach. The current board ships 206 verified assertions across 25 dependency-free check scripts, part of a larger running ledger; no geometric claim enters a sheet until its check passes, and the interactive sheets re-run their checks live.

The synthesis is not of the same grade. The prose that frames the geometry — the placement, the analogies, the naming — is careful draft, not proof. This distinction is load-bearing: across this project, several times a wrong framing passed the automated checks and was caught only by eye or by rendering, while the arithmetic was never the problem. Verification is sound for what it is set up to prove and silent about whether the right thing is being proved. Weight the verified core accordingly, and weight the framing sentences as revisable.

What is new, and what is borrowed

Almost none of the objects are new. The literature is clear on this, and it is better known now than from a referee. Reading 2 — the congruence used as rays — is fully established in computer vision. Reading 1’s convergent solid is classical relief perspective. The congruence itself is a nineteenth-century object. What appears not to be stated anywhere is the symmetry that relates them: one congruence, two roles, the drawn armature as the shared invariant, and the precise two-level agreement structure — drawn lines coincide on the degenerate catalog, full maps only at the corners. And one object does appear genuinely new: the constructed-projection interior, the bent-ray region reached by no projection matrix, which is not a camera and not a limit of cameras.

So the contribution, stated at its true stature: the named projections were all known; the duality showing the glide and the cross-slit to be one congruence read from opposite sides, with the drawing as invariant, is what is new — provisionally. This is not a revolution in geometry. It is a clean unifying reframe carrying one apparently-original theorem and one novel object-class. Stating it at that level is both accurate and the better strategy: it invites verification rather than deflation.

Where it sits

Three bodies of work describe the same underlying objects — line congruences — from three different motives. The nineteenth century built the pure theory (Plücker, Klein, the classification of congruences). The descriptive-geometry tradition built relief perspective: solids engineered to read correctly from a viewpoint. The recent computer-vision community built the general-linear-camera taxonomy: the classification of all two-dimensional linear families of rays. This board connects the three through the role-swap. It borrows the vocabulary of the oldest, recovers the objects of the newest, and revives the construction of the middle one with a single precise change: replace relief perspective’s central viewpoint with an orthographic flatten, and you get bent rays and the constructed interior.

There is a structural reason the duality may be genuinely unclaimed. Vision approached these objects entirely from the ray side — a camera collects rays — and had no reason to ask what the same congruence does when read as edges. Descriptive geometry approached them from the shape side — how to build a solid that reads correctly — and had no reason to read a shaped solid as a bundle of rays. The two literatures barely cite one another. The duality falls in the seam between them, which is the most characteristic possible location for a real-but-unnoticed idea — and a vantage available precisely to someone building a making-system on the seam rather than working inside either field.

Where it is exposed

Three walls, one caveat, one asymmetry — stated plainly, because the scope is part of the claim.

The scope walls. The construction is axis-aligned (the directing axes are perpendicular and parallel to the picture plane), single-station (one shared viewpoint), and charted on r ∈ [0, 1]. What is rigorously covered is therefore the perpendicular-axis, single-station, [0,1] corner of a larger space. General skew two-slit congruences, multiple stations, and the full projective parameter range are frontier, not yet covered.
The reconnaissance caveat. The literature placement rests on a handful of searches — reconnaissance, not a review. The single most important piece of due diligence before any priority claim is the German descriptive-geometry literature (Fiedler, Wiener, the Relieffperspektive tradition), which is under-digitised and under-cited in English vision work, and is exactly where a duality remark could be hiding. Absence of a prior source here is encouraging, not probative.
The verification asymmetry. The checks guard arithmetic and internal consistency; they cannot guard against proving the wrong thing. The correction loop that catches framing errors is human-cranked. This is why the verified core and the synthesis prose carry different weight, and why the fair reading of any capstone sentence here is “true as far as the checks reach, revisable in its framing.”

What it is for

Not for teaching drawing. Its value is threefold. Conceptually, it organises a scattered family under one principle. Potentially, the duality — if it survives a full literature search — is a publishable result at the seam of computational line geometry and multiperspective rendering. Generatively, the constructed-projection interior is a space of images no camera can produce — a representational resource rather than a curiosity. That the making-system and the mathematical unification turn out to be the same object seen from two sides is a quiet echo of the inversion itself.

The frontier

The deepest open lead is the vector / one-form duality, and it is more than a rhyme. Reading 1 measures depth by counting piercings of the parallel depth-plane stack — one-form behaviour, to the letter of the standard differential-geometry picture. Reading 2 measures by incidence at points and lines — the vector side. The two are genuinely the point↔plane duality on the Klein quadric, with the focal lines exchanging. Promoting that from resonance to an exact functorial statement is unfinished, and it is the highest-value unfinished thing on the board: it would give the inversion a second external anchor as deep as the line-geometry one — connecting it to wholly standard differential geometry — and it would express the two agreement levels as the two components of a single pairing.

This introduction makes no new geometric claim; it summarises a verified stack and cites its checks by name. The load-bearing results live in check_inversion (the factorization, the shadow identity, the fixed points, the two agreement levels) and check_transposition (the inversion as an involution, T² = id, and its two strictly nested fixed loci), standing on the channel, two-plane, cross-slit-frame, axiom and rate-square checks beneath them. The whole is a bridge between three literatures built separately, with one new room the bridge makes reachable. Its stature is a clean synthesis with a possible original result — and its scope walls are named above so the reach is not mistaken for more than it is.
