The other way to pull a station point apart. Sheet 37 split it into two slits at two depths on the same axis of sight — a cross-slit. This sheet splits it into two perpendicular armature axes: a vanishing axis that meters depth and a directing axis that assigns direction (Glide Drawings, pp. 88–90). One control: at k = 0 the map is identically the pinhole at Zp; as k rises, each vertical plane of orthogonals keeps its own vanishing point sliding out along the horizon, while each horizontal plane converges toward a directing point beyond the plane of infinity — intercepts coupled by 1/k + 1/c = 1, so the image of infinity opens from a point into a line. A second slider now seats that armature: u₀ slides the directing axis — and with it the crossing on the vanishing axis — anywhere across the width, the same directing-axis-position control as on the construction widget. At k = 0 that seat is the station point, stepping sideways; at k = 1 it ghosts out of the image entirely, because parallel directions have no seat.

Read the trade against the cross-slit. Both spend perspective's depth fidelity to buy breadth — but differently. The cross-slit halves the splay and pays with bowed depth lines and a drifting gauge; the glide takes the splay all the way to 0% while its depth lines stay dead straight and its gauge square images square at every k — no anamorphic correction. The price is honest too: at k = 1 width stops encoding depth altogether (only height recedes), and the far wall never pinches — try the deep corridor, which turns into a parallel slot and clearly belongs to perspective. The house plan bird's-eye and the site relief are the glide's home ground — plans stay plans.

The morph is exact: u = u₀·p(Zg) + (X − u₀)·(k·p(Zg) + (1−k)·p(Z)), v = Y·p(Z) with p(Z) = −Zp/(Z−Zp) — width anchored true at the reference plane Zg, exactly the book's staging rectangle laid over the plan. The u₀ slider seats the directing axis across the width (world-anchored, so it travels with the inverted image). At k = 0 the map is the pinhole whose station point sits at (u₀, 0, Zp) to machine precision — the sheet merely re-hung by a constant; at k = 1 the axis ghosts out and the image does not move at all; at every k and every u₀ the gauge plane stays pinned. Orthogonals draw straight to their vanishing point at û₀ + k·(x̂ − û₀) on the horizon — the two axes cross at û₀ — and continue to a directing point at c·ŷ on the directing axis, 1/k + 1/c = 1. …
